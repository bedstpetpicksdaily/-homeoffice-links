How Do I Stop My Back From Hurting When Sitting All Day?

To stop your back from hurting when sitting all day, adjust your chair so your hips are slightly higher than your knees, support your lower back's natural inward curve with proper lumbar support, and stand up to move every 30 minutes. Most desk-related back pain comes from a combination of poor posture, inadequate chair support, and prolonged static loading of your spinal discs — all three are fixable.

The Short Answer

Back pain from sitting is usually caused by your pelvis rolling backward, which flattens your lumbar spine and puts up to 40% more pressure on your spinal discs than standing does. Fix it by getting a chair with adjustable lumbar support that fills the gap in your lower back, setting your seat height so your feet rest flat with knees at or slightly below hip level, and positioning your monitor at eye level so you're not craning forward. Then break up the sitting itself — a two-minute walk every half hour resets the pressure on your discs and reactivates the muscles that stabilize your spine. No single fix works alone; the combination of proper support plus regular movement is what actually eliminates the pain.

The Full Explanation

Your spine has a natural S-curve. When you stand, that curve distributes your body weight efficiently through your vertebrae and discs. The moment you sit down — especially in a chair without proper support — your pelvis tilts backward, your lumbar curve flattens into a C-shape, and the load shifts from your bones onto the soft tissue of your discs and the ligaments of your lower back.

Research from spinal biomechanics studies has consistently shown that slouched sitting produces significantly higher intradiscal pressure than standing. Hold that position for eight hours a day, five days a week, and you get exactly what millions of desk workers report: a dull, persistent ache in the lower back that gets worse as the day goes on.

There are four specific mechanisms at work:

  • Posterior pelvic tilt. Without lumbar support, your pelvis rotates backward and your lower back rounds. This is the single biggest contributor to sitting-related back pain.
  • Muscle deactivation. Prolonged sitting switches off your glutes and deep core stabilizers. When those muscles go quiet, your lower back muscles and passive spinal structures take over the job of holding you upright — and they fatigue quickly.
  • Hip flexor shortening. Sitting keeps your hip flexors in a shortened position for hours. Over time they tighten and pull on your lumbar spine, increasing the arch and compressing the facet joints.
  • Static loading. Discs have no direct blood supply. They get nutrients through movement, which pumps fluid in and out. Sit motionless for hours and your discs are effectively starved and compressed.

Notice that only one of these is about posture in the moment. The other three are about equipment and movement patterns. That's why "just sit up straight" is terrible advice — it's a muscular effort you can't sustain for eight hours. What you need is a setup that makes good posture the path of least resistance.

The correct sitting position looks like this: feet flat on the floor (or a footrest), knees at roughly 90 degrees or slightly open, hips level with or slightly above your knees, lower back in contact with lumbar support that fills the natural curve, shoulders relaxed and back, elbows at 90 to 100 degrees with forearms roughly parallel to the floor, and the top third of your monitor at eye level about an arm's length away.

If your current chair can't get you into that position, no amount of willpower will fix your back.

What the Experts Say

Occupational health researchers and physical therapists have largely converged on the same conclusion: the problem isn't sitting itself, it's static sitting. Dr. Stuart McGill, one of the most cited spine biomechanics researchers in the world, has argued for years that the human spine is designed for varied loading, and that any single posture held too long becomes harmful regardless of how "correct" it is.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration's ergonomic guidance for computer workstations emphasizes adjustability above all else — a chair should adapt to the user, not the reverse. OSHA specifically calls out lumbar support that matches the inward curve of the lower back, adjustable seat height, and seat depth that leaves two to four inches between the seat edge and the back of your knees.

Physical therapists commonly recommend the "20-8-2" pattern popularized by ergonomics researcher Alan Hedge: for every 30 minutes, spend about 20 minutes seated, 8 minutes standing, and 2 minutes moving. Studies of sit-stand workstations have found meaningful reductions in self-reported lower back discomfort when workers alternate positions rather than committing to either one.

The consensus is clear. Get supportive, adjustable equipment. Then change position regularly. Everything else is a refinement.

Quick Tips

  • Set a 30-minute movement timer. Stand, walk to another room, or do 10 bodyweight squats. Two minutes is enough to reset disc pressure and reactivate your glutes.
  • Raise your monitor to eye level. A screen that's too low pulls your head forward, and every inch of forward head posture adds roughly 10 pounds of leverage load down your spine.
  • Stretch your hip flexors daily. A 30-second kneeling lunge stretch per side directly counteracts the shortening that pulls on your lumbar spine.
  • Stop sitting on your wallet, and keep your feet flat. Crossing your legs or sitting on a thick wallet tilts your pelvis asymmetrically and creates one-sided lower back strain within hours.

Conclusion

Back pain from sitting all day isn't inevitable — it's the predictable result of an unsupportive chair combined with hours of unbroken stillness, and both variables are entirely within your control. Fix your lumbar support first, build a movement habit second, and most desk-related back pain resolves within a couple of weeks.
