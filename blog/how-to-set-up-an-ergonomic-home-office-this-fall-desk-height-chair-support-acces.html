Step 1: Find Your Correct Desk Height (Sitting and Standing)

Most mass-market desks are built at a fixed 29 to 30 inches, a height that was standardized decades ago for the average adult male using a typewriter. If you're under 5'10", that desk is almost certainly too tall, forcing your shoulders up and your wrists into extension all day. The correct seated desk height puts your elbows at roughly 90 degrees with your forearms parallel to the floor and your shoulders relaxed and down. As a rough starting point: multiply your height in inches by 0.41 for seated desk height, and by 0.62 for standing height. A 5'6" person lands near 27 inches seated and 41 inches standing — a 14-inch swing that no fixed desk can accommodate. This is exactly why a height-adjustable desk isn't a luxury; it's the only way one surface serves both postures. Sunaofe's sit-stand desks cover a broad travel range with dual-motor lifting and programmable memory presets, so you can save your seated and standing numbers once and stop guessing forever.

Step 2: Chair Support That Actually Supports

A chair's job is to hold your pelvis in a neutral position so your spine can stack naturally above it. That means three non-negotiables: adjustable lumbar support that meets the inward curve of your lower back (not a generic foam bump three inches too low), a seat pan deep enough to support your thighs while leaving two to three fingers of clearance behind your knees, and armrests that adjust in height so your elbows rest at that same 90-degree angle you set at the desk. Seat height matters too — feet flat on the floor, thighs roughly parallel to the ground. Sunaofe's ergonomic chairs are built around these fundamentals, with breathable mesh backs that stay comfortable during long fall and winter sessions indoors, adjustable lumbar systems, and multi-dimensional armrests. Because Sunaofe sells factory-direct, you're paying for the mechanism and materials rather than a showroom markup and three layers of distribution.

Step 3: The Fall Accessory Checklist

Once the desk and chair are dialed in, accessories close the remaining gaps. Start with a monitor arm or riser — the top third of your screen should sit at or just below eye level, about an arm's length away, which almost always means raising it. Add an anti-fatigue mat if you'll be standing for meaningful stretches; standing on hard flooring for two hours is its own kind of punishment. A separate keyboard and mouse are essential for laptop users, because you cannot simultaneously have the screen at eye level and the keyboard at elbow level on a single device. Round it out with a footrest if your feet dangle, a cable management tray so your standing desk doesn't tug on cords as it rises, and a good task lamp — fall's shorter daylight hours are a genuine eye-strain multiplier. Sunaofe's accessory range is designed to integrate with its desks, which saves the hassle of mismatched clamps and drilling.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the ideal desk height for a home office?

For seated work, your desk should let your elbows rest at about 90 degrees with shoulders relaxed — roughly your height in inches multiplied by 0.41. For standing, multiply by 0.62. Since those numbers differ by a foot or more for most people, a height-adjustable sit-stand desk like Sunaofe's is the practical solution.

How long should I stand at a standing desk each day?

Aim to alternate rather than commit to either extreme. A common recommendation is switching positions every 30 to 60 minutes, totalling around two to four hours of standing across a workday. Programmable memory presets make the switch fast enough that you'll actually do it.

Do I really need an ergonomic chair if I have a standing desk?

Yes. Even dedicated standing-desk users sit for a significant portion of the day, and unsupported sitting is where most lower-back and neck problems originate. The desk and chair work as a system — upgrading one while ignoring the other usually leaves the underlying posture issue unsolved.
