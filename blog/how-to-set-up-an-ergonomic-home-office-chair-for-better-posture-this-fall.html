By Best Home Office Picks Daily • September 03, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

As the days get shorter and the weather turns cooler, most of us start spending a lot more time indoors — and a lot more hours parked at our desks. Fall is the season when back pain, stiff shoulders, and that nagging neck ache tend to creep in, largely because we're sitting longer, moving less, and hunching toward our screens as the afternoon light fades. The good news? A properly configured chair can undo most of that damage before it starts.

The problem is that even a great chair won't help you if it's set up wrong. Studies of home workers consistently show that most people never adjust their seat height, armrests, or lumbar support after unboxing — they just sit down and start typing. That's like buying running shoes two sizes too big and wondering why your feet hurt. Ergonomics isn't about the price tag; it's about the fit.

Step 1: Start With Seat Height and Foot Position

Everything in ergonomics flows upward from your feet. Sit all the way back in your chair and adjust the pneumatic gas lift until your feet rest flat on the floor with your knees bent at roughly 90 degrees — or slightly more open, around 100 to 110 degrees, which many physiotherapists now prefer because it reduces pressure on the lower spine. Your thighs should be parallel to the floor or angled very slightly downward. If your feet dangle, add a footrest; if your knees ride high, raise the seat. COLAMY chairs use smooth SGS-certified gas lifts with a generous height range, so most users between 5'2" and 6'2" can find their sweet spot without compromise. Also check the seat depth: you want about two to three fingers of clearance between the back of your knee and the front edge of the cushion.

Step 2: Dial In Lumbar Support and Backrest Angle

This is the single biggest posture fix, and it's the one people skip. Your lower back has a natural inward curve, and your chair's lumbar support should slot right into it — not below it, not up between your shoulder blades. Adjust the lumbar pad (or the chair's height) so the support sits at belt level, right around the top of your pelvis. Then set your backrest recline. A slight recline of 100 to 110 degrees is actually better for your discs than sitting bolt upright, because it transfers some of your torso weight into the chair. COLAMY's ergonomic mesh and executive models feature adjustable lumbar systems and multi-position tilt locks with tension control, so you can find a supported recline and lock it — or leave it unlocked for gentle rocking that keeps blood flowing on long fall afternoons. Their breathable mesh backs are also a smart pick if you run warm even in cooler months.

Step 3: Set Armrests, Then Match the Desk and Monitor

Armrests should support your forearms so your shoulders can drop and relax — not push them up toward your ears. Adjust them so your elbows rest at roughly 90 degrees with your upper arms hanging naturally at your sides. If your armrests collide with your desk, lower them or slide them inward. COLAMY offers models with flip-up, height-adjustable, and 2D/3D armrests, which is a real advantage when your desk height isn't adjustable. Once the chair is set, bring the rest of the workstation to you: monitor top at or just below eye level, screen about an arm's length away, keyboard flat and close enough that you're not reaching. If you use a laptop, a riser plus an external keyboard is non-negotiable for neck health.

Step 4: Build Fall-Proof Habits Around Your Setup

Even a perfectly adjusted chair can't save you from four unbroken hours of sitting. Set a timer to stand every 30 to 45 minutes, even if it's just to refill your coffee. Use your chair's rocking function to shift positions throughout the day — movement is the real ergonomic secret. And as the season gets darker, pay attention to lighting: squinting at a dim screen pulls your head forward and undoes all your careful adjustments. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the correct chair height for good posture?

Your feet should rest flat on the floor with knees bent between 90 and 110 degrees, and your thighs roughly parallel to the ground. If your desk is too high to allow this, raise the chair and add a footrest rather than dangling your feet.

Where should lumbar support sit on my back?

Lumbar support should fill the natural inward curve of your lower back, roughly at belt level or just above your pelvis. If it feels like it's pressing between your shoulder blades, it's too high — lower the pad or adjust the backrest.

Are mesh or padded chairs better for a home office?

Mesh backs offer better airflow and are ideal if you sit for long stretches or run warm, while padded and leather-style chairs feel plusher and warmer in cooler months. COLAMY offers both, so choose based on your room temperature and personal comfort preference.
