What to Look For

  • Full Range of Motion (Height, Tilt, Swivel, Rotation): Your monitor needs to move up and down by at least 5-7 inches, tilt backward 15-20 degrees, and rotate to portrait mode if needed. This flexibility lets you position the screen at your exact eye level—the top of the monitor should sit at or slightly below eye height when you're sitting naturally upright.
  • Adequate Weight Capacity and Smooth Adjustability: Look for arms rated for 10-27 pounds that use gas springs or smooth tension mechanisms, not wobbly friction locks. You want one-handed adjustments that hold position without drifting throughout the day. Budget models that feel stiff or squeaky will frustrate you into poor habits.
  • Cable Management and Desk Space Preservation: Choose an arm with integrated cable channels and a compact base (or clamp design) that doesn't consume valuable desk real estate. Why This Works for This Situation

Back pain from desk work isn't really about your chair—it's about your monitor forcing you into a forward head posture. Every inch your head moves forward puts an additional 10 pounds of stress on your cervical spine and neck muscles. When your monitor sits too low, you crane downward. A monitor arm solves this at the source by making it effortless to position the screen exactly where it should be: eye level, about an arm's length away, perpendicular to windows (to reduce glare).

A good monitor arm lets you tilt the screen slightly, rotate it between landscape and portrait, or raise it higher when you're doing video calls.
