What Is the Correct Monitor Height for Good Posture?

The correct monitor height places the top of your screen at or slightly below your eye level when you're sitting upright, with the center of the screen roughly 15 to 20 degrees below your horizontal line of sight. For most adults, this means the top edge of the monitor sits somewhere between 40 and 45 inches from the floor when seated in a properly adjusted chair.

Getting this measurement right is one of the single highest-impact changes you can make to your workspace. It costs almost nothing to fix, takes about five minutes, and directly reduces the neck strain, shoulder tension, and upper-back fatigue that plague most desk workers.

The Short Answer

Position your monitor so the top of the screen is level with or 1 to 2 inches below your eyes when you're seated with good posture. Your eyes should naturally fall on a point about 2 to 3 inches below the top edge of the display. The screen should also sit an arm's length away — roughly 20 to 30 inches from your face. If you have to tilt your head up or crane it forward to read text comfortably, your monitor is in the wrong place.

The Full Explanation

Monitor height matters because your head is heavy. An adult head weighs 10 to 12 pounds in a neutral position, but the effective load on your cervical spine multiplies as your neck tilts forward. At a 15-degree forward tilt, your neck supports roughly 27 pounds of force. At 45 degrees, that figure climbs to around 49 pounds. Hold that position eight hours a day, five days a week, and you get the tight traps, headaches, and burning sensation between the shoulder blades that send people to physical therapists.

A monitor that sits too low forces exactly this forward head tilt. Laptop users suffer the worst version of this problem, because a laptop screen attached to a keyboard can never be at the right height and the right typing distance at the same time. It's a physical impossibility — one of the two will always be compromised.

A monitor that sits too high creates a different problem. Looking upward requires you to open your eyelids wider, which increases tear evaporation and causes dry, irritated eyes. It also encourages you to tip your head back, compressing the joints at the base of your skull. This is why the guidance is "at or slightly below eye level" rather than simply "high."

To set your height correctly, work from the bottom up. First, adjust your chair so your feet rest flat on the floor and your knees sit at roughly 90 degrees. Second, adjust your desk or chair so your elbows are at about 90 degrees when your hands are on the keyboard, with your shoulders relaxed and down. Only then should you set your monitor height — because moving your chair afterward invalidates everything.

Once you're seated properly, close your eyes, let your head settle into a neutral position, then open them. Wherever your gaze naturally lands should be about one-third of the way down from the top of your screen. Adjust from there.

A few variables shift these numbers. If you wear progressive or bifocal lenses, you likely need the monitor 1 to 3 inches lower than standard, because those lenses require you to look through the lower portion of the glass — otherwise you'll tilt your head back all day to find the reading zone. If you use an ultrawide or a 32-inch-plus display, prioritize the eye-level rule for the top edge and increase your viewing distance to reduce how much you swivel your neck. If you run dual monitors and use both equally, angle them in a shallow V with the inner edges meeting directly in front of you. If one screen is clearly primary, put that one dead center and place the secondary off to the side.

What the Experts Say

OSHA's computer workstation guidance recommends positioning the monitor so the top of the screen is at or slightly below eye level, with the viewing angle falling between 15 and 20 degrees below horizontal. The agency specifically notes that the screen should be positioned to avoid excessive neck flexion or extension in either direction.

The Mayo Clinic offers a practical version of the same rule: place the monitor directly in front of you, about an arm's length away, with the top of the screen at or just below eye level. Their office ergonomics guidance emphasizes that the monitor should be squarely in front of the keyboard, not offset to one side — a mistake that quietly introduces spinal rotation into every working hour.

Ergonomics researchers have also documented what's sometimes called the "resting gaze angle" — the natural downward tilt of the eyes when the head is in a relaxed, neutral position. Studies consistently place this angle between 10 and 15 degrees below horizontal, which is why the slightly-below-eye-level recommendation outperforms a perfectly level screen. Your eyes prefer looking slightly down; your neck strongly prefers not tilting.

Physical therapists who treat desk workers tend to add one caveat to all of this: no single posture is good posture if you hold it for eight straight hours. The correct monitor height reduces cumulative load, but movement is what actually prevents the injury. Static loading is the underlying problem, and geometry alone can't solve it.

The Product Solution

Most monitors ship with stands that offer minimal height adjustment, and most laptops offer none at all. Stacking books or reams of paper works in a pinch, but it's unstable, non-adjustable, and wastes the desk space underneath.

An adjustable monitor stand riser solves the problem directly. These sit on your desk and lift the screen 3 to 8 inches, with adjustable models letting you dial in the exact height rather than guessing. The better ones include a storage shelf underneath for a keyboard, notebook, or laptop, which reclaims the desk real estate you gave up.

When choosing one, check three things. First, the height range — measure the gap between your current screen top and your eye level, then confirm the riser covers it. Second, the weight capacity, especially for larger displays or if you're stacking a laptop plus an external monitor. Third, the footprint, since a riser that's wider than your desk depth allows will push the screen too close to your face.

For laptop users, a riser is close to mandatory. Elevating the laptop to eye level and pairing it with an external keyboard and mouse is the only way to get both the screen and the hands in correct positions simultaneously. Adjustable models are worth the small premium over fixed-height versions because your ideal setting will change if you switch chairs, add a standing desk, or upgrade to a different monitor size.

Quick Tips

  • Adjust your chair first, monitor second. Setting screen height before your seated position is correct means you'll have to redo it. Feet flat, elbows at 90 degrees, then the monitor.
  • Use the eyes-closed test. Sit up, close your eyes, relax your neck, then open them. Your gaze should land about a third of the way down the screen. Adjust until it does.
  • Lower the screen if you wear progressive lenses. Drop it 1 to 3 inches below the standard recommendation so you're reading through the correct part of the lens without tipping your head back.
  • Move every 30 minutes regardless. Perfect monitor height still can't offset eight hours of stillness. Stand, walk, or roll your shoulders on a timer.

Conclusion

The correct monitor height is simple to remember: top of screen at or just below eye level, an arm's length away, directly in front of you. Spend five minutes measuring it today, and your neck will notice the difference within a week.
